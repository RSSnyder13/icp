---
name: icp
description: Turn a product idea, sales call, pile of notes, or existing company into a testable "hell yes" hypothesis of who would be weird NOT to buy it, or tear down the user's own buyer hypothesis and propose a better one, then map every hypothesis by how many people fit it and how likely they are to be stuck right now. Use when the user asks who their ideal customer, ICP, or target buyer is, wants to pressure-test a buyer hypothesis, asks "who would buy this?", is doing customer discovery for a startup idea, or wants to compare several ideas or buyers by how big and how urgent the market is.
---
 
# What this skill is for
 
The user will almost always bring an idea, not a buyer. Ideas start on the supply side: what the product is ("an AI sales assistant"). Your job is to take them to the demand side, by answering one question:
 
**What are the characteristics of someone who'd be weird not to buy this?**
 
For example, for "an AI sales assistant," someone who'd be weird not to buy is someone who can't hire an actual sales assistant right now, but has things they need a sales assistant to do and can't do themselves (for example, because their volume of sales or non-sales work is increasing).
 
Assume that zero people who COULD buy a product will. People who would get value, have the pain, or would see ROI are not buyers. A buyer is someone for whom not buying is not an option. For everyone else, not buying is an option, and they won't buy.
 
Someone can't not buy only when both of these are true right now:
 
1. They are trying to do something they can't not do.
2. They can't do it with any of their existing options.
If either is false, it would be weird if they bought.
 
# The four checks (your private working test)
 
Every hypothesis, yours or the user's, is judged on exactly these four, and nothing else. You use them to build and test hypotheses. They are NOT shown to the user as a per-hypothesis breakdown.
 
- **Can't do nothing.** Doing nothing is not acceptable to them. This can come from a threat, a deadline, or their own drive to get something done. The test is what they're already doing: they're actively trying and hitting a wall, not just interested. Someone who keeps pushing after being blocked counts. Someone who would welcome it if it were easy does not.
- **Can't not do X.** X is a specific thing they must get done. Not a goal, a standard, or a "would like to." If they could do X at a worse level and live with it, X fails.
- **Can't keep doing what they've been doing (if they're doing X some way today, which is usually true).** If they are already doing X some way today, that way has stopped working or can't continue, for a specific reason. The procurement CEO's team wasn't cutting costs. The Lemlist buyer used Apollo and failed. Only skip this check if you've confirmed they aren't doing X in any way today.
- **Can't do X with Y.** Y is every other relevant, specific existing option: doing it themselves, hiring someone, the named tools on the market, services, agencies, consultants, the generic AI tools. Each one must be ruled out with a reason.
## What counts as a real block on Y
 
Use the examples below as the test. Do not invent your own. All of these count as real blocks:
 
- A rule or requirement that rules the option out (financial advisors and compliance).
- A decision the buyer has made and won't reverse, like refusing to hire more people (the procurement CEO).
- Having tried an option and failed with it, or finding it unusable (Lemlist buyers and Apollo).
- The option costing unreasonable time or money for the situation (SOC 2 before Vanta).
- Not trusting an option to do the job, when the reason is specific.
Do not dismiss a stated block as "just a preference." If the user says the buyer won't hire, won't use Claude, or won't use a tool, treat it like the procurement CEO's refusal to hire: a real block. The only question is whether every relevant option in Y was named.
 
# Examples
 
These are the calibration for every judgment you make.
 
**"An AI notetaker for financial advisors."** Financial advisors are desperately trying to not take meeting notes, but must take them (compliance), and can't use any generic AI notetaker on the market (compliance). Not buying is not an option.
 
**"AI that helps you rip and replace data from your competitors."** Not "a VP of sales who wants to increase win rate." Instead: a VP of sales who must increase win rate, and the only way to do it is to win more deals where rip-and-replace is required, and who can't use a paid white-glove service and isn't allowed to offer a free white-glove migration.
 
**"SOC 2 compliance software" (before Vanta existed).** A CEO or sales leader whose enterprise deal is on hold because the customer demanded SOC 2, where a traditional consulting firm would take an unreasonable amount of time and money.
 
**"Lemlist."** Someone who can't not do outbound and can't use Apollo or other sequencers for a specific reason, like finding them too hard to use. They either used those tools and failed, or couldn't use them.
 
**Reflow.** They first targeted HR leaders with "AI that handles employee questions." HR leaders can just do nothing: occasionally inconvenienced, never stuck. They switched to CEOs of PEOs (outsourced HR firms), who had to cut costs and raise margins and customer satisfaction, and had no good way to do it. Those CEOs were stuck.
 
**"Bookkeeping for small restaurant owners."** Not "an owner who'd like cleaner books." Instead: an owner who is actively trying to open a second location, has already been turned down by the bank because their financials can't be trusted, and keeps going back. They can't fix the books themselves, their part-time bookkeeper only does data entry, and a full accounting firm costs more than they can spend before the loan comes through. No deadline or threat forces it; their own push to grow does, and they're already stuck mid-push.
 
**The procurement software.** It cut 99% of the effort of running RFPs, but procurement teams were rarely under the gun to save money. The CEOs were stuck: their teams weren't cutting costs, and they refused to hire more people. So the product was reshaped into a service that ran RFPs fast. The CEOs didn't want software, because their team would have to use it, and their team wasn't an option.
 
# Procedure
 
## Step 1: Private working (never shown to the user)
 
Do all of this in your head before writing anything:
 
1. Figure out the thing this idea helps someone get done (the idea-level X), and list every existing option someone could use for it today, including doing nothing, doing it themselves, hiring, named tools, services, agencies, and generic AI.
2. Name what people most commonly do about X today, especially anything the product is built on, improves, or would replace. Consider at least one candidate who is already doing that and hitting a wall with it. It only survives if it passes every check like any other candidate.
3. Draft the general criteria (Step 2's three bullets) for this idea.
4. Think laterally before settling on hypotheses. Try at least several of these angles:
   - A different person in the chain: the end user, their boss, the owner, the agency or service provider who does this for others, the platform or franchise that sits above many of them.
   - A specific event that forces action right now: a deal on hold, a lost customer, a deadline, a rule change, a sudden drop in something they depend on.
   - Someone who already tried the obvious options and failed with them.
   - Someone actively pushing to do more (grow, launch, expand) who keeps getting blocked by a boss, board, lender, or funder until they can do X.
   - Someone who is forbidden from, or has refused, the obvious options.
   - Someone for whom the cheap or DIY option stopped working because of scale or volume.
   - Someone for whom the expensive option is priced for a bigger buyer.
   - Whether the product would fit better if it were a done-for-you service instead of a tool (the procurement example).
5. For every candidate hypothesis, run it against all four checks. Every option in Y needs a reason it's ruled out. X must be a must-do, not a nice-to-have. Throw out or fix any candidate that fails.
6. Keep only the strongest hypotheses (usually two or three) that pass every check that applies.
## Step 2: Write the response
 
The whole response is the sections below, in this order. Nothing before, between, or after them. No summary, no commentary on positioning, pitch, copy, or wording, no advice on how to test, no evidence sections, no per-hypothesis breakdown of the checks.
 
If the user gives more than one idea, write Sections 2 to 5 once per idea (under a short heading with the idea's name), and write Section 1 and Section 6 only once for the whole response. Section 6 then plots every hypothesis from every idea on the same map.
 
**Section 1: What this is**
 
Always open with this text, word for word:
 
> This skill is not giving you a hypothesis of who COULD buy — where buying is optional. Because if they can not buy, they probably won't. Instead, this skill is to get you the rough contours of a hypothesis of who would be weird NOT to buy — where buying is non-optional. These people will pull the product out of your hands.
 
**Section 2: The "buying isn't optional" criteria**
 
Three bullets, filled in for this specific idea:
 
- **Prioritizing** {the idea-specific thing they're trying to get done} **right now**
- **MUST prioritize this** for some reason (e.g., {2–4 concrete, idea-specific examples of what forces it: an event, a rule, a loss, a deadline})
- **CAN'T do this with available options** (e.g., {2–4 concrete examples naming the real existing options and why they'd be ruled out, including whatever they're doing today})
These are general contours for the idea, not tied to one hypothesis.
 
**Section 3: Teardown (only if the user gave a specific buyer)**
 
One short paragraph in plain words saying where their buyer meets the criteria and where it breaks (e.g., they can do nothing, or they can use an existing option). Do not call something broken unless the examples above would. If they left out relevant options, name them. Skip this section entirely if the user only gave an idea.
 
**Section 4: Hypotheses**
 
Each hypothesis gets a short bold label and then one paragraph describing the person who'd be weird not to buy: who they are, what's happening to them right now that forces action, what they must get done, what they've been doing and why it stopped working (if relevant), and why each other option is off the table. Write it so the user could recognize and go find these people. Prose, not bullets.
 
**Section 5: Reshape (only if needed)**
 
Only if no one can meet all the checks for the product as described: one or two sentences on how to change the product's shape so someone can't not buy it, following the procurement example. Otherwise leave this out.
 
**Section 6: The map**
 
Plot every hypothesis from Section 4 on two axes. This sizes the people who passed. It never decides who passes; only the four checks do that, and a hypothesis that failed the checks is never put on the map, no matter how big it would look.
 
- **Across: how many people fit the description.** Count the people who match who they are (the role, the kind of company, the setup they have), ignoring whether they're stuck right now. "Founders of early B2B startups who still do the selling themselves," not "founders with a stalled deal."
- **Up: how likely one of those people is to be in the stuck situation right now.** Out of the people counted across, what share are in the forcing moment on any given week: the deal on hold, the spike of leads, the lender's no. Something that happens to most of them most of the time sits high. Something that happens once every few years sits low.
Multiplying the two gives roughly how many people are stuck right now, which is the number of people who'd be weird not to buy today. Top right is many people, often stuck. Bottom left is few people, rarely stuck.
 
How to estimate:
 
- Give each axis as a rough range, in whole orders of magnitude for people (hundreds, thousands, tens of thousands, hundreds of thousands, millions) and as a plain share for likelihood (e.g., "about 1 in 10 at any time").
- Give one short line per hypothesis saying how you got each number, using a countable anchor where you can (number of licensed advisors in the US, number of seed-stage startups raised last year, how often a typical deal stalls). If the user gave you real numbers (their list size, their customer counts), use those over general knowledge.
- For likelihood, think about how often the forcing moment happens and how long it lasts. A stuck state that lasts two weeks and happens twice a year means about 1 in 12 at any time.
- These are guesses, and the response says so once, plainly. Never add false precision.
- Keep the same scale across every hypothesis and every idea in the response, so points can be compared.
How to show it:
 
- If an inline visual or chart tool is available, draw a scatter plot: people across on a log scale, likelihood up, one labeled point per hypothesis, colored by idea when there's more than one idea. Add faint diagonal lines for "stuck right now" (e.g., 100, 1,000, 10,000 people) so the user can read the product at a glance.
- Always also give a small plain table, one row per hypothesis: short label, how many people, how likely right now, roughly how many stuck right now, and the one-line basis. If no visual tool is available, the table is the map.
- After the table, at most two sentences pointing out what the map shows (e.g., which point has the most people stuck right now, or that one hypothesis is small but almost always stuck). No recommendation beyond what the map shows unless the user asked for one.
# Rules
 
- Use only what the user gave you in this request, plus general knowledge of the existing options on the market. Do not pull in outside context (past conversations, memory, other files) unless the user asks you to.
- Judge whether someone passes only on the four checks. Do not grade the idea, the market, or the business. The map in Section 6 sizes the hypotheses that passed; it is not a grade and never overrides the checks.
- Plain language. No jargon or terms of art.
- Never show the four checks, the lateral-thinking list, or the per-hypothesis testing to the user.
# Check before sending
 
If any answer is no, fix the response before sending.
 
1. Does the response open with the exact Section 1 text?
2. Are the Section 2 criteria filled in with concrete, idea-specific examples, including named existing options?
3. Is every hypothesis a single paragraph, with no visible per-hypothesis checklist?
4. Did every hypothesis pass all four checks that apply in my private working, with every relevant option ruled out for a specific reason?
5. Is X in every hypothesis something they must get done or are already actively trying and failing to get done, not a standard they'd like to hit?
6. Did I avoid critiquing the product, pitch, or wording?
7. Did I include a teardown only if the user named a buyer, and a reshape only if no one meets all the checks?
8. Is every hypothesis from Section 4 (and only those) on the map, on one shared scale, with a one-line basis for both numbers?
9. Did I state once that the map's numbers are rough guesses, and avoid false precision?
